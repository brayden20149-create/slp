Side-by-Side Clinical Writing Analysis

Review how non-compliant writing compares to professional, reimbursable clinical documentation. All clinical samples below are rendered strictly in Times New Roman.

Clinical Example 1: Daily Session SOAP Note

Pediatric Recept/Expressive Language
Bad Example (Vague & Non-Compliant)
S: Johnny was in a bad mood today and cried a little when his mom left.

O: Played with the farm toys. He did okay with his words today. We worked on requesting animals. He got about 50% right.

A: Johnny had a hard time focusing. I think he was tired.

P: Work on farm animals again next week.

Deficiencies: Subjective speculation ("bad mood", "tired"), unmeasurable data ("did okay"), lacks specific prompt levels or targeted linguistic goals, non-reimbursable for insurance.

Good Example (Professional & Reimbursable)
S: Patient (Client J., age 4;2) transitioned into the clinic room with mild initial separation anxiety, resolving within 3 minutes upon introduction of tactile visual schedules.

O: Client produced 2-3 word utterances (e.g., "want big cow", "more pig please") to request preferred toy items in 12/15 opportunities (80% accuracy) given minimal verbal prompts.

A: Client demonstrated improved expressive phrase length (+20% from baseline) when provided structured visual choices.

P: Continue POC targeting 3-word utterance expansion using visual sentence strips; fade verbal cues to independence.

Strengths: Measurable objective data (80% accuracy in 12/15 trials), clearly specified prompt levels (minimal verbal prompts), objective behavior tracking, reimbursable clinical synthesis.

Clinical Example 2: Diagnostic Assessment Impression

Pediatric Articulation & Phonology
Bad Example (Vague & Non-Compliant)
Assessment Summary:
Maya is a sweet 5-year-old girl who came in for speech evaluation. She is very hard to understand when she talks fast. She drops sounds at the end of words. Standardized testing was done and she scored low in speech. She needs speech therapy twice a week to talk better.

Deficiencies: Omits test names, standard scores, percentile ranks, specific phonological processes (e.g., final consonant deletion), and functional communicative impact.

Good Example (Professional & Reimbursable)
Assessment Impression:
Administration of the Goldman-Fristoe Test of Articulation-3 (GFTA-3) yielded a Standard Score of 68 (Percentile Rank: 2nd, -2.1 SD), indicating a moderate-to-severe phonological disorder. Primary processes identified include Final Consonant Deletion (occurring in 85% of opportunities) and Fronting (/k, g/ -> /t, d/). Conversational speech intelligibility was rated at 55% to unfamiliar listeners, significantly impeding functional academic and social interactions. Skilled SLP intervention is recommended 2x/30 min weekly.

Strengths: Standardized score reporting, exact statistical deviations, precise phonological process classification, and clear functional impact justification for medical necessity.

Literacy & Writing Theory

Joyce Walker’s CHAT Framework in SLP

Applying Cultural-Historical Activity Theory (CHAT) to understand speech-language pathology documentation as dynamic, interconnected literate activities.

1

Production

How documentation is physically & digitally constructed

Production refers to the tools, technologies, and physical environments used to draft SLP texts. In modern clinical practice, this includes Electronic Medical Record (EMR) software (e.g., Epic, WebPT), point-of-care dictation, standardized testing forms, and clinical shorthand.

SLP Application: Balancing real-time EHR data entry during sessions while maintaining active clinical engagement with pediatric clients.
2

Representation

How clinicians frame clients & speech behaviors

Representation explores how writers depict identity, diagnosis, and ability through text. In SLP documentation, clinicians must use person-first or identity-first language (e.g., "child with autism" vs. "autistic child"), maintaining objective, strength-based tone without deficit bias.

SLP Application: Framing child challenges as "areas for growth with visual supports" rather than labeling performance as "refused or non-compliant".
3

Distribution

How documentation travels & circulates

Distribution examines how texts move across systems, stakeholders, and secure digital networks. A single evaluation report is distributed via encrypted HIPAA portals to pediatricians, school districts, insurance reviewers, and parents.

SLP Application: Formatting reports with clear executive summaries so busy physicians can immediately digest clinical findings.
4

Reception

How diverse audiences interpret & act upon texts

Reception analyzes how readers process and react to documentation. An insurance auditor reads a note to verify medical necessity for reimbursement, while a parent reads the same note seeking hope and clear directions for home practice.

SLP Application: Code-switching documentation styles—providing quantitative metric rigor for insurance while adding parent-friendly home summaries.
Theoretical Synthesis

Why CHAT Matters for Graduate Students & Clinical Fellows

When entering clinical practice, new SLPs often view documentation as an isolated chore. Applying Joyce Walker's CHAT framework reveals that every word written influences client care, funding authorization, and interprofessional trust.

By recognizing the social and structural lifecycle of your writing, you transition from a passive note-taker into an authoritative, empathetic clinical advocate.

Clinical Mentorship Guidance

Frequently Asked Questions

Real-world advice for new speech-language pathologists navigating daily clinical writing, insurance reimbursement, and caregiver communication.

Focus strictly on the "Objective" and "Assessment" nexus. State the target skill, the exact quantitative data (e.g., % accuracy, trials completed), and the specific cueing level provided (e.g., minimal visual, moderate tactile). Avoid long narrative descriptions of non-therapeutic play.

Key takeaway: Payers want to see skilled intervention. Document what you as the SLP provided that a layperson could not.

The most frequent error is writing subjective judgments without behavioral backing—such as writing "Client was uncooperative" instead of "Client engaged in task avoidance by pushing materials away in 4/5 attempts." Always describe observable behaviors objectively.

Key takeaway: Objective documentation protects the clinician legally and provides accurate baseline data for team members.

Format every goal with 5 core components: [Timeframe] + [Client Name] + [Specific Target Skill] + [Quantitative Condition/Prompt Level] + [Measurement Criterion over X consecutive sessions]. Example: "By December 2026, Client will produce initial /s/ blends in sentences with 80% accuracy given minimal visual cues across 3 consecutive sessions."

Key takeaway: Avoid vague phrases like "will improve speech articulation".

Use the "Translate & Actionize" model. First, state the goal in plain language (e.g., replacing "final consonant deletion" with "ending sounds on words like cat and dog"). Second, share a celebrated victory (e.g., "Sam remembered his ending sounds in 8 out of 10 words today!"). Third, give 1 simple home strategy (e.g., "When reading at bedtime, emphasize the last sound in words together").

Key takeaway: Caregivers don't need complex medical terminology to be effective therapy partners—they need clear, encouraging, and functional home activities.
